Act now: call for help

  • ST elevation, or a STEMI equivalent, with chest pain
  • VT, VF, polymorphic VT or torsades de pointes
  • Complete AV block, Mobitz II, or pauses over 3 s with symptoms
  • Fast, irregular, wide QRS: think pre-excited AF
  • Signs of hyperkalaemia
  • QTc 500 ms or more
  • Brugada type 1 pattern with syncope
  • Any brady- or tachycardia with low blood pressure, chest pain or confusion

Education only. Follow your guidelines and local protocols. Treat the patient, not the ECG.

1Read every ECG the same way

  1. Check the basics. Right patient, date and time. Calibration 10 mm/mV, speed 25 mm/s.
  2. Rate. See card 2.
  3. Rhythm. Regular? A P wave before every QRS? A QRS after every P?
  4. Axis. See card 3.
  5. P wave. Upright in I and II, negative in aVR? Size and shape.
  6. PR interval. Short, normal or long? Constant?
  7. QRS. Width, shape (bundle branch block?), Q waves, voltage.
  8. ST segment. Elevated or depressed? Which leads?
  9. T wave (and U wave). Inverted, peaked, flat?
  10. QT and QTc.
  11. Compare with the previous ECG and with the patient in front of you.

Lead check: negative P wave and QRS in lead I with a positive aVR? Think limb electrodes swapped (or dextrocardia). Repeat the ECG.

1 small square1 mm = 40 ms = 0.1 mV
1 large square5 mm = 200 ms = 0.5 mV

Rate

  • Regular: 300 ÷ large squares between two R waves (or 1500 ÷ small squares).
  • Large squares 1-2-3-4-5-6 = 300-150-100-75-60-50/min.
  • Irregular: number of QRS complexes in the 10 s strip × 6.

4AV blocks

First degree
PR > 200 ms. Every P wave is conducted.
Second degree, Mobitz I (Wenckebach)
PR lengthens beat by beat until one P wave is not conducted. The PR after the pause is the shortest. Usually in the AV node.
Second degree, Mobitz II
Constant PR, then a P wave suddenly not conducted. Usually below the AV node, often with a wide QRS. Usually needs a pacemaker.
2:1 block
Every second P wave not conducted. One strip can't tell type I from type II; a wide QRS points below the node.
Third degree (complete)
P waves and QRS complexes beat independently; more P waves than QRS. Escape rhythm narrow (junctional, 40–60/min) or wide (ventricular, 20–40/min).

Look for hidden P waves in the T waves and the QRS. March them out with calipers.

5Bundle branch and fascicular blocks

Right bundle branch block QRS ≥ 120 ms

  • rsR′ ("M") in V1–V2
  • Wide, slurred S wave in I, aVL, V5–V6
  • QRS 110–119 ms with this pattern: incomplete RBBB

Left bundle branch block QRS ≥ 120 ms

  • QS or rS in V1
  • Broad, notched or slurred R in I, aVL, V5–V6
  • No septal q waves in I, V5–V6
  • ST–T goes opposite to the QRS (this is expected)

Left anterior fascicular block QRS < 120 ms

Axis −45° to −90°. qR in aVL, rS in II, III and aVF.

Left posterior fascicular block QRS < 120 ms

Axis +90° to +180°. rS in I and aVL, qR in III and aVF. Rule out other causes of right axis first.

Bifascicular block: RBBB plus either fascicular block. Add a long PR and syncope: think of conduction disease that may need pacing.

7Don't miss: equivalents and look-alikes

Treat like STEMI

Posterior MI
ST depression in V1–V3 with upright T waves. Record V7–V9.
de Winter pattern
Upsloping ST depression with tall, symmetric T waves in V1–V6. Proximal LAD occlusion.
LBBB or paced rhythm with ischaemic symptoms
Sgarbossa: concordant ST elevation ≥ 1 mm, concordant ST depression ≥ 1 mm in V1–V3, or discordant ST elevation ≥ 25% of the S wave depth (Smith).

High risk, not STEMI

Widespread ST depression + ST elevation in aVR
Left main or multivessel disease, or global subendocardial ischaemia.
Wellens pattern
Biphasic or deeply inverted T waves in V2–V3 in a pain-free patient: critical proximal LAD stenosis.

Look-alikes

Early repolarisation, pericarditis, LVH, LBBB, ventricular pacing, Brugada pattern, hyperkalaemia, LV aneurysm, Takotsubo.

Clues for VT

  • AV dissociation; capture or fusion beats
  • Concordance: all precordial QRS positive or all negative
  • QRS > 160 ms; extreme axis (−90° to ±180°)
  • Initial R wave in aVR
  • No RS complex in any precordial lead

9Electrolytes, drugs, temperature

High potassium
Tall peaked T waves → longer PR, flat P → wide QRS → sine wave, bradycardia, VF or asystole. The ECG can look normal despite a high level.
Low potassium
Flat T waves, prominent U waves, ST depression, long QU interval.
Calcium
Low: long QT through a long ST segment. High: short QT.
Digoxin
Effect: scooped ("reverse tick") ST depression. Toxicity: almost any arrhythmia, classically atrial tachycardia with block or bidirectional VT.
Hypothermia
Osborn (J) waves, bradycardia, long intervals, shivering artefact.
Long QT
QTc ≥ 500 ms: high risk of torsades. Check drugs, potassium, magnesium and calcium.

10Patterns to recognise

WPW pattern
PR < 120 ms, delta wave (slurred QRS start), wide QRS, secondary ST–T changes.
Brugada type 1
Coved ST elevation ≥ 2 mm in V1–V2 (also in high positions), followed by a negative T wave.
Pericarditis
Widespread concave ST elevation, PR depression (PR elevation in aVR), no reciprocal ST depression except in aVR and V1.
Pulmonary embolism
Most often just sinus tachycardia. Also: RBBB, right axis, T inversion in V1–V4 and III, S1Q3T3 (neither sensitive nor specific).
Left ventricular hypertrophy
Sokolow–Lyon: S in V1 + R in V5 or V6 ≥ 35 mm. Cornell: R in aVL + S in V3 > 28 mm (men), > 20 mm (women). Strain: ST depression and T inversion in lateral leads.
Right ventricular hypertrophy
R ≥ 7 mm or R/S > 1 in V1, right axis, strain in V1–V3.

11Pacemaker ECG

  • Spikes: before the P wave (atrial pacing), before the QRS (ventricular pacing). Bipolar leads give tiny spikes: look in every lead.
  • Right ventricular pacing: wide QRS with an LBBB-like shape, ST–T opposite to the QRS (expected).
  • Biventricular pacing: often an R wave in V1 and a negative QRS in I.
  • Conduction system pacing (His or left bundle area): a narrower QRS.
Mode letters1: chamber paced · 2: chamber sensed (A, V, D, O) · 3: response (I inhibit, T trigger, D both, O none) · R: rate response. Examples: VVI, AAI, DDD.
